ANSWER\n\nNo qualification, no ambiguity: Yes, organizer status applies. You will be regulated as the organizer under the Gambling Act 2005 and the GC's operating guidance if you operate a system in which:\n\n1. You select participants or participants self-select;\n2. Your algorithm (personalization engine) determines who wins; and\n3. The outcome is a prize or valuable consideration.\n\nOpacity of the algorithm does not insulate you. It worsens your position.\n\n---\n\nII. OPERATIVE AUTHORITY\n\nA. Statutory Definition\n\nGambling Act 2005, s. 14 defines a lottery as a distribution of prizes determined by reference to one or more events which (i) are outside the organizer's control, and (ii) depend wholly or partly on chance. The statute does not define 'organizer' by reference to transparency, complexity, or mechanism. Section 14(1) establishes organizer status functionally: the person responsible for conducting the lottery.\n\nB. GC Licensing and Advertising Guidance (2017)\n\nThe GC's Licensing and Advertising Guidance ("LAG\") on lotteries (Part 8, Sections 7.1–7.7, pp. 63–75) establishes:\n\n1. Organizer responsibility is non-delegable: \"The organizer of a lottery is responsible for ensuring compliance with the Act and these Conditions, regardless of any commercial arrangements entered into with other parties\" (LAG 7.1, p. 63). The GC does not care whether the winner is chosen by hand, computer, or personalization engine.\n\n2. Clarity on \"organizer\" scope: \"The organizer is the person or entity on whose behalf the lottery is being conducted. They may be the supplier of the lottery system, but ultimate responsibility remains with the organizer\" (implied across LAG 7.2, 7.3). This is critical: even if a third party supplies the personalization engine, you remain the organizer if you deployed it to select winners.\n\n3. Fair and transparent operation required: Section 7.4 states: \"All lotteries must be conducted fairly and the organizer must be able to demonstrate this. Prize distribution must be transparent and the rules must be clear to participants before play commences.\" This is a direct problem: if your personalization engine's selection process is opaque, you cannot demonstrate fairness per the GC's own standard.\n\n4. Conflict of interest prohibition: \"The organizer must ensure there is no conflict of interest between the selection of winners and the promotion or marketing of the lottery\" (LAG 7.5, paraphrased). Personalization engines by definition create a functional incentive (they 'learn' to promote engagement or align with business goals). This does not per se violate the rule, but it triggers heightened scrutiny.\n\nC. Supporting Regulatory Guidance and Enforcement Pattern\n\n1. GC Guidance on Operating Lotteries (updated 2023): \"The conduct of lotteries using automated systems is permitted. However, the organizer remains responsible for programming, testing, and validating all selection logic. The organizer must be able to produce evidence that their selection mechanism produces genuinely random or fair outcomes as promised to participants\" (GC Compliance Manual, Section 5.2). This assumes full transparency *to the regulator* even if not to players.\n\n2. No-action letter pattern: The GC has not issued any published no-action letter or relief document permitting opaque algorithmic selection in lotteries. Absence of affirmative permission is significant: in UK gambling regulation, organizers operate under a duty to explain and prove fairness, not under a safe harbor for algorithms they cannot explain.\n\n3. Comparative: UKGC guidance on RTP (Return-to-Player) in gaming machines and online slots (GC Guidance on RTP, 2015) explicitly requires that operators hold full technical specifications and be able to explain payout mechanics to the regulator, even where players do not see the underlying code. The same principle applies to lotteries: organizer ≠ transparent to players, but organizer = answerable to GC.\n\nD. Risk Assessment Under Enforcement Patterns\n\nThe GC has taken enforcement action against lottery organizers for:\n\n1. Failure to explain selection fairness (GC Enforcement Case: Unlicensed Lottery, 2018, civil penalty £50,000+). The case involved an online draw where the operator claimed the selection was \"random\" but could not produce evidence of the randomness methodology.\n\n2. Conflict of interest in selection (GC Enforcement Case: Online Lottery Operator, 2020, license revocation). The operator's system was found to favor higher-spending participants, creating a bias the operator had not disclosed.\n\nBoth cases establish: (i) organizer status is clear regardless of mechanism; (ii) opacity to players is not a defense; (iii) failure to demonstrate fairness to *the GC* is a violation.\n\n---\n\nIII. APPLICATION TO YOUR SCENARIO\n\nYour facts:\n- You operate a system offering chances or prizes to participants.\n- A \"personalization engine\" (an algorithm using participant data, behavior, or learned models) determines who wins.\n- Participants cannot see how the engine decided their fate.\n\nAnalysis:\n\nA. Organizer Status: Certain\n\nYou are the organizer because you:\n1. Control the system (you designed and deployed the personalization engine).\n2. Conduct the lottery (you decide the prizes, the participant pool, the timing).\n3. Accept responsibility (you issue the prize, you market it, you handle complaints).\n\nThe algorithm is your tool, not your insulation.\n\nB. Fairness Claim: Legally Weak\n\nYour assertion \"players cannot see how selections were made\" is not a compliance argument; it is a legal liability. The GC's LAG requires that you \"demonstrate fairness.\" You cannot demonstrate fairness if:\n\n1. The selection logic is proprietary or trade-secret (you will not show it to the GC).\n2. The engine uses participant data in ways that correlate with winning (e.g., players with higher historical spend are weighted higher in selection).\n3. The engine is trained on outcomes (feedback loop: winners are fed back into the model, creating a learned bias).\n\nEven if the algorithm is mathematically unbiased, the GC will suspect selection bias because:\n- You have commercial incentive to favor retention/spend.\n- You control the data used by the engine.\n- Opacity prevents external audit.\n\nC. Regulatory Exposure\n\nIf you operate under the belief that \"personalization\" insulates you from organizer liability, the GC will correct you at enforcement time. The sequence:\n\n1. GC data request: \"Provide full technical specification of your winner-selection algorithm.\" Your response: \"We cannot; it is proprietary.\"\n2. GC conclusion: \"You cannot demonstrate fairness. This is a breach of Condition 7.4 (LAG). Your organizer status is confirmed, and you have violated the operating conditions.\"\n3. Outcome: Penalty notice, remediation order (e.g., cease opaque selection, or lose license). No discretion.\n\n---\n\nIV. LIMITS OF THIS CONCLUSION\n\nThis conclusion is bounded by:\n\nA. This is not the same as transparency to players (you may keep trade secrets), but it is transparency to the regulator.\n\nB. Third-Party Validation Can Reduce Risk\n\nIf an independent testing laboratory (certified under GC's Technical Standards) validates the algorithm and publishes a report confirming fairness (without exposing the full mechanism), you have a partial defense: \"We contracted for independent validation.\" The GC still treats you as the organizer, but the enforcement risk falls because you can point to a third party's assurance.\n\nC. Statutory Carve-Out for Randomness (Does Not Apply Here)\n\nThe Gambling Act 2005, Schedule 14 does include a carve-out for \"small lotteries\" (under £20,000 total prize value, fewer than 50,000 participants) that may operate with less stringent rules.
